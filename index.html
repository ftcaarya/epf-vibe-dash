Component 1 · The Data

The Trendline

What the graph shows

This graph shows the U.S. unemployment rate from 2019 to 2026, with the post-pandemic period shaded. Unemployment spiked to record levels in spring 2020 when COVID shut down the economy, then fell quickly to historic lows near 3.5% by 2023 as businesses reopened. Since then, the rate has slowly crept up, reaching a four-year high of 4.5% in November 2025 before easing back to 4.2% by September 2026. The flat line over the past several months shows a "low-hire, low-fire" job market: few people are being laid off, but companies are adding very few new jobs. Overall, the trend suggests the labor market is stable but not improving.

Research Questions

What is the current value?
4.2% in September 2026, with about 7.1 million people unemployed[1].
How does it compare to 1 year ago?
Down 0.2 points from 4.4% in September 2025, which is slightly better[2].
How does it compare to 3 years ago?
Up 0.5 points from 3.7% in September 2023, so the job market is looser than during the post-pandemic boom[2]. (BLS first reported 3.8% for that month[10]; it was later revised to 3.7%.)
What major events affected it?
COVID shutdowns (the 2020 spike)[2], rapid reopening (2021–22), Fed rate hikes cooling hiring (2022–23)[5], the 2025 government shutdown[4] and weak 2025 hiring pushing the rate to a 4-year high of 4.5% in November 2025[2], a February 2026 loss of 92,000 jobs[11], the Iran war oil shock starting in March 2026[9], AI-related hiring freezes, especially for entry-level jobs[12], and the Fed's September 2026 rate hike[7].
Is it improving, declining, or stable?
Stable. BLS says the rate has stayed between 4.1% and 4.3% since March 2026[1]. Hiring is very slow, though: only +29,000 jobs in September, and July and August were revised down by 60,000 combined[1].

Component 2 · The Feeling

The Vibe Meter

Do Americans feel secure in their jobs, or are they worried about finding work?

Today's rating: Cautious

Hover over or tap a zone to see what it means.

Worried signals

  • University of Michigan consumer sentiment was 48.0 in October 2026, a five-month low[15] and not far above May 2026's all-time record low of 44.8[16].
  • Only +29,000 jobs in September; July and August were revised down 60,000[1].
  • Wages (+3.0%) are losing to inflation (3.7%)[1][13].
  • The Conference Board's "jobs plentiful" minus "hard to get" gap is only +5.3%, which is historically weak[17].

Steady signals

  • Unemployment is still low by historical standards at 4.2%[1].
  • Conference Board confidence jumped to 91.7 in October, back to June levels, on better views of jobs[17].
  • Real GDP grew at a strong 3.4% annual rate in Q3 2026[18].
  • Layoffs remain low: 2026 job cuts through September were down 40% from a year earlier[3].

Rating Defense

I rate today's economy Cautious, leaning toward Concern. On paper, the job market looks okay: unemployment is 4.2%[1], layoffs are low[3], and GDP grew 3.4% last quarter[18]. But Americans don't feel secure. Hiring has nearly stalled, wages are rising more slowly than prices, and University of Michigan consumer sentiment sits near the lowest level ever recorded[16]. People who already have jobs are mostly holding on, but people looking for work, especially young and entry-level workers, are struggling[12]. That mix of solid data and anxious feelings is exactly what "mixed signals" means.

Research Questions

How are news organizations describing the economy?
As a "low-hire, low-fire" job market that is stable but stuck[21], with worries about surging energy prices and inflation[3].
Are experts optimistic or worried?
Mostly cautious. Economists see no broad rise in layoffs, but expect the Iran war's high energy prices and supply strains to weigh on the job market by the end of 2026 and into 2027[21].
Do all sources interpret the data the same way?
No. The Fed saw the economy as strong enough to raise rates to fight inflation[7], and two officials wanted another hike in October[25], while many economists see weak hiring as a warning sign[21]. Two consumer surveys even moved in opposite directions in October (UMich down, Conference Board up)[15][17].
Where would you place today's mood and why?
Cautious. See the Rating Defense above.

The Confidence Loop

How a change in unemployment travels through consumer behavior to GDP and back again.

The opposite direction: when unemployment falls, the same steps run in reverse and create an upward spiral. Use the button to switch.

Where are we now? We're in a stalled loop. Unemployment isn't rising fast, but hiring is so slow that confidence is weak. Real GDP still grew 3.4% in Q3 2026[18], so the downward spiral hasn't started.

Component 4 · Optional

The Wealth Effect

When stock prices or home values rise, people feel richer even if their paycheck hasn't changed. Investors and homeowners tend to spend more, which boosts business sales and can lead companies to hire, helping lower unemployment. Through the multiplier effect, one person's extra spending becomes another person's income: a homeowner spends $1,000 on repairs, the contractor spends it at restaurants, and restaurant workers earn more. But the wealth effect mostly helps people who already own stocks or homes; unemployed and lower-income workers benefit less, so it may not fix feelings of job insecurity. Overall, rising wealth slightly strengthens the job market, but it can't fully offset worries about weak hiring and high prices.

  1. Asset values rise
  2. People feel wealthier
  3. They spend $1,000
  4. Contractor earns it
  5. Contractor eats out
  6. Restaurant workers earn more
  7. Businesses hire

Research Questions

How does government spending affect unemployment?
It creates jobs directly (public projects) and indirectly (contractors, suppliers), which lowers unemployment.
How do tax cuts affect it?
They raise disposable income, so people spend more and businesses hire.
How do tax increases affect it?
They reduce spending, which can slow hiring.
What should the government do during a recession?
Use expansionary fiscal policy: raise spending and/or cut taxes.
What should it do during high inflation?
Use contractionary fiscal policy: cut spending and/or raise taxes to cool demand.

Current context

  • The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 2025) is delivering roughly $285 billion of fiscal stimulus in 2026 through tax cuts, nearly 1% of GDP[23].
  • The FY2026 federal deficit came in at about $2.0 trillion (6.2% of GDP), $140 billion more than CBO had projected[24].
  • Inflation is 3.7%, well above the Fed's 2% target[13].

Policy Advisory · Recommendation: Maintain

Maintain overall spending and taxes. No new broad stimulus, but use targeted help. The economy isn't in a recession (GDP grew 3.4%[18] and unemployment is 4.2%[1]), and inflation is already high at 3.7%[13], so a big new round of tax cuts or spending would risk pushing prices even higher, especially since 2026 already got a large boost from tax cuts[23] and the deficit is already $2 trillion[24]. Raising taxes or cutting spending now would also be risky because hiring is so weak. Instead, the government should keep total spending steady while shifting money toward targeted programs: job-training and apprenticeship programs for entry-level workers losing out to AI[12], and support for the 1.9 million long-term unemployed[1]. This helps people find work without overheating the whole economy.

Monetary Policy

The Fed Pitch

Raise, lower, or hold? A recommendation to the Federal Open Market Committee.

Current setting: federal funds rate target 3.75%–4.00%, after a 25-basis-point hike on September 16, 2026[7] (the first hike since July 2023[8]) and a hold on October 28, 2026, by a 10–2 vote, with two members wanting another hike[25]. Fed Chair: Kevin Warsh (confirmed May 2026)[27]. Next FOMC meeting: December 8–9, 2026[26].

Not recommended

Raise rates

Hiring is already near a standstill; higher borrowing costs could push unemployment up.

My recommendation

Hold steady

Balances both halves of the Fed's dual mandate: stable prices and maximum employment.

Not recommended

Lower rates

Inflation rose to 3.7% in September from 3.4%[13]; a cut could make prices worse.

Analysis Framework

What does the indicator suggest?
A stable but sluggish job market, not a crisis[1].
Is the economy overheating?
Not in jobs, because hiring is weak. Prices are rising mainly from energy: core inflation is a lower 2.6%[13].
Is confidence strong or weak?
Weak to mixed (UMich 48.0; Conference Board 91.7)[15][17].
Are consumers spending too much or too little?
About right for now, since GDP grew 3.4%[18], but falling real wages could slow spending soon.
Which action best supports stability?
Holding rates steady.

Recommendation to the FOMC: Hold

I recommend the FOMC hold interest rates steady at 3.75%–4.00% at its December meeting. The Fed has two jobs: keep prices stable and keep employment high. Inflation is too high at 3.7%[13], but much of that comes from oil and energy prices tied to the Iran war[9], and raising interest rates can't produce more oil. Meanwhile, the job market is fragile: only 29,000 jobs were added in September, and earlier months were revised down[1]. Another hike could tip a "low-hire" market into a "high-fire" one and push unemployment up. Cutting rates would be the wrong move too, because it could add fuel to inflation. Holding steady lets the September hike work its way through the economy while the Fed watches the next jobs and inflation reports. If unemployment rises above about 4.5%, the Fed should consider cutting; if core inflation keeps climbing, it should consider another hike.

Government (fiscal)
